\opening{Dear Editors,}

We submit our manuscript ``Cross-Corpus Evaluation of Domain-Aware Query
Reformulation for Regulatory Traceability'' for consideration in
\emph{Empirical Software Engineering}.

The paper reports a negative result and the analysis that establishes it.
Compliance mapping, linking regulatory requirements to the controls that
address them, has few public collections, so methods are usually measured on
benchmarks their own authors build. We built one such benchmark, measured a
substantial benefit from domain-aware query reformulation on it, and then
asked whether that measurement carried. Under a single ranking protocol
applied to four corpora, the $+0.121$ Top-1 gain on our own corpus did not
recur on three externally authored, NIST-derived corpora, which give
$+0.028$, $0.000$ and $+0.032$.

Two properties of those estimates are the more transferable contribution.
Each turns on fewer than ten requirements whose top-1 correctness changes at
all, which no confidence interval conveys on its own, and each moves by up to
$0.029$ when a latent-semantic backend is refitted on a different population,
a choice unrelated to the intervention under test. We report wins, losses and
ties with every paired effect for the first reason, and a three-cell
factorial separating evaluation protocol from fitting population for the
second.

We believe the work fits EMSE for three reasons. It is an empirical study in
requirements engineering and traceability, which the journal's scope covers
explicitly. It is a replication attempt on our own prior result, conducted
under a common protocol rather than by comparing published numbers. And it
ships infrastructure others can extend: four corpora, a frozen analysis
specification, the evaluation harness, and provenance tooling that verifies
every number in the paper against a hashed record.

One analysis, a gated lexical--semantic hybrid, was preregistered on the OSF
before it was run (doi:10.17605/OSF.IO/NZXRV). It returned non-inferiority
with no evidence of superiority, while producing $+0.085$ on one corpus
against $-0.088$ on another. We report it as evidence rather than setting it
aside, and we are explicit that because its hypothesis was generated from
these same corpora it remains preregistered but exploratory. Everything else
in the paper is exploratory and labelled as such.

We are equally explicit about what the study cannot establish. The three
external corpora share a NIST lineage and a single target catalogue, so they
are correlated replications rather than independent samples; with one corpus
of our own construction the design cannot identify which of its properties is
responsible for the difference; and our vocabulary-gap measure is fitted per
corpus, so it provides no scale on which corpora could be ranked. We make no
claim that engineered benchmarks inflate results in general.

An earlier and narrower version of this work, covering two corpora and
concluding that the reformulation benefit was real but conditional on
vocabulary-gap width, was submitted to \emph{Data \& Knowledge Engineering}
and rejected. Two further corpora do not support that conclusion. The present
manuscript is substantially rewritten rather than revised: the research
questions, protocol, estimand, statistical procedure and framing have all
changed, and the earlier conclusion is one this work retracts.

The manuscript is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, and all
authors have approved the submission. We declare no competing interests. The
corpora, harness and analysis code are available at
\url{https://github.com/sumanshusohal/RAA-Compliance-Mapping}.
